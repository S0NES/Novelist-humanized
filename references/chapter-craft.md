# Chapter craft

Openings, structure, hooks, suspense and expansion. Section 2 of `SKILL.md` still governs every sentence you write with these tools.

## The first 20% decides

Readers decide in the first fifth of a chapter whether to keep going. The opening needs:

1. Immediate tension: the reader feels a threat or a conflict.
2. A real event that moves the plot.
3. An emotional hit: curiosity, shock or worry.
4. A reason to read the next page.

### Openings to rewrite on sight

| Opening | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Weather ("The sky was clear that morning...") | Unrelated to the story |
| Waking-up routine | No conflict |
| Recap of the last chapter | The reader already knows it |
| A briefing on the city or its history | Information dump, no action |
| Small talk | No tension |
| Explaining causes | Tells instead of showing |

### Ten opening techniques

Use one or combine two.

1. **In media res.** Start at the peak of the action. *The shot took the vase behind his ear. He rolled behind the sofa without looking back.*
2. **Abnormal situation.** Something that can't be. *The dead man sat at his desk, finishing a report.*
3. **Shocking line.** *"I sold the boy," his wife said, and turned a page of her magazine.*
4. **Countdown.** Time pressure from the first line. *Three minutes on the timer, and the kit was one red wire short.*
5. **Major discovery.** A clue that reframes earlier events.
6. **Crisis.** The worst moment for the character. *The door gave on the second kick. The safe was still open.*
7. **Mystery.** An unexplained phenomenon. *He woke with a tattoo on the back of his hand that he had never seen.*
8. **Betrayal.** Trust breaks. *"Sorry," said the partner he had trusted for ten years, and pressed the barrel to his spine.*
9. **Hard choice.** A decision with no good option.
10. **Flash-forward.** A future moment, then back. *In three days everyone in this room would regret the vote. For now they were smiling.*

Self-test after the draft. If any answer is no, rewrite the opening:

1. After three paragraphs, would a reader keep going?
2. Is there conflict or danger?
3. Is there a surprise or turn?
4. Does the reader feel something strong?
5. Did you avoid every opening in the table above?

## Chapter structure

| Part | Share | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Opening hook | about 20% | Tension, event, emotion |
| Development | about 55% | New information, a relationship shift, escalation or growth |
| Climax | about 15 to 20% | The action, emotional or psychological peak of the chapter |
| Closing hook | last 5 to 10% | A reason to open the next chapter |

Avoid in development: pure scenery, interactions that don't touch the plot, dialogue that repeats what the reader knows.

Inside the chapter, pace moves: tension, relief, new tension, more tension. Example: the hero is chased, hides in a safe house, finds the safe house already searched, has to run now.

Alternate density. High density is action, fast dialogue and quick events. Low density is interior thought, setting and emotional settling. Too much high density tires the reader; too much low density bores them.

Every chapter needs at least one core event that can't be cut. If an event can be removed without hurting understanding, remove it.

### Chapter types

- **Plot-driving.** Clear progress or an important reveal.
- **Character-deepening.** Background, motive, inner conflict. It must tie to the main line.
- **Atmosphere.** Builds a mood or tension: the calm before a storm, a stakeout, waiting for a verdict.
- **Transition.** Connects two major events. Keep it short.
- **Lore.** Follows the exposition rule in `SKILL.md`: two characters, a still place, a long conversation, no action.

## Closing hooks

Every chapter ends on a hook. Ten types:

1. **Sudden reveal.** Information that changes everything. *The last message on the dead man's phone came from his own number, sent three days ago.*
2. **Imminent crisis.** Danger the next chapter has to face at once.
3. **Interrupted action.** *His finger was on the trigger when someone behind him said, "Don't."*
4. **Identity reversal.** Someone is not who we thought.
5. **Dilemma.** The chapter ends before the choice.
6. **Mysterious object or clue.** *The safe held one photo, taken yesterday: her, asleep, shot through the window.*
7. **Deadline.** A time limit with too few resources.
8. **Promise or threat.** *"By midnight everyone will know what you did ten years ago." The email had no other line.*
9. **Impossible disappearance.** *He looked away for one second. The cell was empty, the cuffs still locked.*
10. **A line with a hidden meaning.** *"Congratulations," the interviewer said. "Just as good as your sister." She was an only child.*

### Suspense rules

- **No false suspense.** Footsteps that turn out to be the cat cheat the reader.
- **No deus ex machina.** A solution must be planted earlier and logged in the ledger.
- **Don't leave too much open.** Answer at least one old question per chapter, then raise a new one.
- **No low-stakes hooks.** "He didn't know what to eat for dinner" is not a hook. "Someone had poisoned his dinner" is.
- **Escalate.** Each new question should go deeper than the last: who stole the file, then the thief is a colleague, then the colleague is an agent, then the agent knows the hero's secret.
- **Keep several threads alive:** the main mystery, a character's identity, a relationship, a deadline.

### Suspense intensity

| Level | Reader thinks | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Interesting." | Middle chapters |
| 2 | "What happens next?" | Middle chapters |
| 3 | "He has to act now." | Climax chapters |
| 4 | "Will he survive?" | Climax and pre-ending |
| 5 | "What does it all mean?" | End of the book |

Overall intensity rises across the book, with dips.

### Foreshadowing

Plant small details early that the reader will overlook. Pay them off later with a mirrored scene (similar situation, different outcome), a repeated line in a new context, or an object that comes back.

## Expansion

When a chapter is short, expand only with material that serves the story:

- Concrete detail of the space: layout, light, smell, texture, how people move in it.
- Interior life shown, not told: hesitation, a memory of one or two paragraphs, a moral argument with oneself.
- Dialogue with subtext, probing and a real detour that comes back.
- The senses: sight, sound, touch, smell, taste.
- A side thread: a supporting character's small story, a planted clue, a shift in a relationship.
- Slowing a key moment down beat by beat.

Every expansion keeps tension and points at the main line. Expanded text obeys section 2 of `SKILL.md` like everything else.

## Story structures for planning

| Story type | Structure | First-arc length |
|---|---|---|
| Adventure, fantasy | Hero's journey | 8 to 12 chapters |
| Mystery | Mystery structure | 8 to 10 |
| Romance | Romance structure | 6 to 10 |
| Thriller | Thriller structure | 5 to 8 |
| Psychological, twist | Twist structure | 6 to 8 |
| Many protagonists | Multi-thread | 10 to 15 |

- **Three acts.** Setup about 25% (status quo, inciting incident, decision to act). Confrontation about 50% (new world, midpoint reveal, escalation, betrayal). Resolution about 25% (lowest point, climax, new normal).
- **Mystery.** The puzzle appears; the investigation deepens and suspects lie; a second event reveals a pattern; the detective is in danger; a reversal; the final confrontation; the case closes with one question left.
- **Romance.** Meet badly; forced together; attraction grows; a secret or obstacle pushes them apart; the break; they overcome it.
- **Thriller.** Crisis, a plan that fails, a chase into a corner, a narrow chance, a final fight, consequences.
- **Twist.** Establish the "truth"; plant doubts; a small twist; a second twist; a final twist that recolors everything. Every twist must be fair in hindsight.
- **Multi-thread.** Alternate threads or timelines. They must converge, and early unrelated events must turn out to connect.
